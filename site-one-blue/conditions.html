What is treated, and how it is found

Gastrointestinal conditions announce themselves through symptoms that overlap: bloating, heartburn, abdominal pain, constipation, jaundice. Below is what those symptoms usually turn out to be, and the procedures that tell them apart.

  • GastroscopyOesophagus, stomach and duodenum
  • ColonoscopyThe full length of the large bowel
  • TherapeuticBleeding, polyps, strictures, bile ducts
  • LiverHepatitis, fatty liver, abnormal blood tests

Conditions

Common conditions

Stomach & duodenum

Peptic ulcers

Open sores in the lining of the stomach or the first part of the small bowel, causing gnawing or burning upper abdominal pain that often changes with meals. Most are caused by H. pylori infection or by long term use of anti inflammatory painkillers. Untreated they can bleed or perforate; treated, the great majority heal completely.

Whole bowel

Irritable bowel syndrome

Recurring abdominal pain with bloating and a changed bowel habit, where the bowel wall itself looks normal. It is real, it is common, and it is diagnosed properly, by first excluding coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease and other causes that look identical from the outside. Management combines diet, targeted medicines and review over time.

Immune inflammation

Inflammatory bowel disease

Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis inflame and ulcerate the bowel wall, producing diarrhoea, blood, urgency, pain and weight loss. Diagnosis needs endoscopy with biopsies, and often imaging. Treatment aims at remission, getting the inflammation to settle and keeping it settled, with regular monitoring rather than treatment only during flare ups.

Screening matters

Colon cancer

Nearly all colon cancers begin as polyps, small growths that cause no symptoms for years. Colonoscopy finds polyps and removes them during the same examination, which is why screening prevents cancer rather than only detecting it. Screening usually starts at fifty, and earlier where a close relative has been affected.

Liver

Hepatitis and liver disease

Inflammation of the liver from viral infection, alcohol, medicines, or fat deposited in the liver. It is frequently silent, showing up first as an abnormal blood test rather than as jaundice. Establishing the cause is the whole task. Viral hepatitis is treatable, fatty liver responds to metabolic management, and both benefit from being found early.

Also seen

Reflux, coeliac disease, gallstones and more

Acid reflux and hiatus hernia, coeliac disease, gallstones and biliary pain, pancreatitis, difficulty swallowing, chronic constipation and diarrhoea, unexplained iron deficiency anaemia, and abnormal liver blood tests are all routine gastroenterology work. If your symptom is not listed, it is still worth bringing.

Procedures

Advanced diagnostic and therapeutic endoscopy

Every procedure below is performed by Dr. Firoz himself, with light sedation and monitoring. Findings are explained the same day, in plain language, with written results.

See all services

  • Gastroscopy

    A thin camera passed through the mouth to examine the oesophagus, stomach and the first part of the small bowel. It answers questions about reflux, swallowing difficulty, ulcers, upper abdominal pain and anaemia, and takes biopsies for coeliac disease and H. pylori in the same pass.

    Usually 5 to 15 minutes

  • Colonoscopy

    Examination of the full length of the large bowel. It is the definitive test for rectal bleeding, changed bowel habit, chronic diarrhoea and iron deficiency anaemia, and the screening test for bowel cancer. Polyps found during the examination are removed as they are found.

    Usually 20 to 45 minutes

  • Therapeutic endoscopy

    Where the camera treats as well as looks: stopping a bleeding ulcer or varix, removing polyps, stretching a narrowed oesophagus, placing stents, and relieving blocked bile ducts. This is the work that turns an emergency admission into a same day treatment.

    Varies by procedure

  • Liver assessment

    A structured assessment of abnormal liver blood tests or suspected liver disease: history, viral serology, metabolic screening, imaging, and liver biopsy where the diagnosis needs it. The aim is a named cause, not a description.

    Clinic based, in stages

Preparing

How to prepare, and what to bring

Before a gastroscopy

  1. Nothing to eat for six hours before the appointment. Clear fluids may be allowed up to two hours before, and you will be told which.
  2. Keep taking your regular medicines unless told otherwise. Blood thinners and diabetes medicines need specific instructions.
  3. Bring someone who can take you home. Sedation makes driving unsafe for the rest of the day.
  4. Bring your current medicine list and any previous endoscopy or scan reports.

Before a colonoscopy

  1. Start the low residue diet on the day given to you, usually the day before the procedure.
  2. Take the bowel preparation exactly as timed. A clean bowel is what makes the examination accurate.
  3. Stay near a bathroom during preparation, and keep drinking clear fluids so you do not become dehydrated.
  4. Arrange a companion for the journey home, and plan a quiet rest of the day.

These are general guides. Your written instructions from the clinic always take priority, because preparation changes with the procedure and with the medicines you take.
