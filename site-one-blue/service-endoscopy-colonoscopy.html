Endoscopy and colonoscopy

Helping you understand your digestive health, with expert assessment at The Mombasa Hospital.

Book a consultation

Digestive problems such as persistent stomach pain, heartburn, difficulty swallowing, changes in bowel habits and rectal bleeding are common. While many of these symptoms have simple causes, some may require further investigation.

Endoscopy and colonoscopy are important procedures that allow doctors to examine the digestive system directly. They can help identify the cause of symptoms, diagnose conditions and, in many cases, provide treatment.

Both procedures are commonly performed and are generally safe when carried out by trained professionals.

What is an endoscopy?

An upper gastrointestinal endoscopy, commonly called a gastroscopy, allows the doctor to examine the upper part of your digestive system.

A thin, flexible tube with a small camera and light at its tip is gently passed through your mouth into your food pipe (oesophagus), stomach and the first part of your small intestine.

The images appear on a screen, allowing the doctor to examine the lining of these organs carefully.

Why might I need an endoscopy?

Your doctor may recommend an endoscopy if you have:

  • Persistent heartburn or acid reflux.
  • Recurrent upper abdominal pain or discomfort.
  • Difficulty or pain when swallowing.
  • Persistent nausea or vomiting.
  • Unexplained weight loss.
  • Vomiting blood or passing black, tarry stools.
  • Suspected stomach ulcers or inflammation.
  • Unexplained anaemia or suspected bleeding from the digestive tract.

An endoscopy can help diagnose conditions such as acid reflux disease, gastritis, stomach or duodenal ulcers, and certain growths or cancers.

How is an endoscopy performed?

You will usually be asked not to eat or drink for a specified period before the procedure.

A local anaesthetic spray may be used to numb your throat, and sedation may be offered to help you relax and feel more comfortable.

The doctor gently passes the flexible tube through your mouth while examining your digestive tract. The procedure usually takes around 5 to 15 minutes, although this may vary.

You may experience temporary discomfort, gagging or bloating, but the procedure is generally well tolerated.

If necessary, the doctor can take a small tissue sample, called a biopsy, for laboratory examination. This is usually painless.

Most patients go home the same day.

What is a colonoscopy?

A colonoscopy allows the doctor to examine the inside of your large intestine, also known as the colon.

A thin, flexible tube with a camera is gently passed through the anus into the large intestine. The camera provides detailed images of the bowel lining.

Colonoscopy is one of the most useful tests for investigating bowel symptoms and detecting abnormalities such as polyps, inflammation and colorectal cancer.

Why might I need a colonoscopy?

Your doctor may recommend a colonoscopy if you have:

  • Blood in your stool or bleeding from the rectum.
  • A persistent change in bowel habits, including diarrhoea or constipation.
  • Unexplained abdominal pain or bloating.
  • Unexplained iron deficiency anaemia.
  • Unintentional weight loss.
  • Suspected inflammatory bowel disease, such as Crohn’s disease or ulcerative colitis.
  • A family history of colorectal cancer or certain bowel polyps.
  • A need for colorectal cancer screening, depending on your age and risk factors.

A colonoscopy can help identify the cause of symptoms and detect bowel abnormalities at an early stage.

How should I prepare for a colonoscopy?

Good preparation is essential because the doctor needs to see the bowel lining clearly.

You will usually be asked to:

  • Follow specific dietary instructions before the procedure.
  • Take prescribed bowel cleansing medicine to empty the colon.
  • Drink the recommended amount of clear fluids.
  • Follow instructions regarding your regular medicines.

Tell your doctor if you take blood thinning medicines or medication for diabetes, or if you have any significant medical conditions. Do not stop prescribed medication without medical advice.

If the bowel is not adequately cleaned, some abnormalities may be difficult to see and the procedure may need to be repeated.

What happens during a colonoscopy?

The procedure is usually performed with sedation to help you relax and improve your comfort.

You lie on your side while the doctor gently passes the colonoscope through the anus and examines the large intestine. Air or carbon dioxide is introduced to expand the bowel and improve visibility.

You may experience some pressure, bloating or cramping, but these symptoms are generally temporary.

If polyps are found, they can often be removed during the same procedure. Tissue samples can also be taken for laboratory examination.

The procedure usually takes around 20 to 45 minutes, depending on the findings and any treatment required.

Most patients return home the same day. If sedation has been used, you will need someone to take you home and should follow your healthcare team’s instructions about driving and other activities.

What should I expect before, during and after the procedure?

Knowing what to expect can help you feel prepared and reassured. Your healthcare team will give you instructions specific to your health and the procedure you are having.

Before the procedure

Before an endoscopy or colonoscopy, you will have an opportunity to discuss your symptoms, medical history, allergies and regular medicines with the doctor or nurse. Tell the team if you are pregnant, have heart or lung problems, take blood thinners or medicines for diabetes, or have had problems with sedation or anaesthesia in the past.

For an endoscopy, you will usually need to stop eating and drinking for a specified period so that your stomach is empty.

For a colonoscopy, you will need to follow the recommended diet and take bowel cleansing medicine as instructed. You may have frequent loose bowel movements while your bowel is being emptied. Drink the recommended clear fluids to help prevent dehydration, unless your healthcare team gives you different instructions.

Ask whether you need to change or temporarily stop any regular medicines. Do not make changes without medical advice. If sedation is planned, arrange for a responsible adult to take you home and stay with you as advised.

During the procedure

On arrival, the team will check your details, explain the procedure and answer any final questions. You will be asked to provide consent before the procedure begins.

You may receive a spray to numb your throat for an endoscopy and sedation for either procedure, depending on your needs and the hospital’s arrangements. Your blood pressure, pulse and oxygen levels may be monitored throughout.

The doctor will gently pass the endoscope through your mouth for an endoscopy or the colonoscope through the anus for a colonoscopy. You may feel pressure, bloating, mild cramping or temporary discomfort, but the team will monitor you and help you remain as comfortable as possible.

Biopsies can be taken during either procedure, and polyps can often be removed during a colonoscopy. These steps are usually painless, and the samples are sent to a laboratory for examination.

After the procedure

You will rest in a recovery area until the effects of any sedation begin to wear off. You may have a mild sore throat after an endoscopy or temporary bloating and cramping after a colonoscopy. These symptoms usually improve within a short time.

The doctor may discuss the initial findings with you before you leave. Laboratory results from biopsies or removed polyps may take several days or longer. You will be told how and when you will receive these results and whether follow up is needed.

If you have received sedation, you should not drive, operate machinery, drink alcohol or make important decisions for the period advised by your healthcare team. Arrange for someone to take you home and follow all discharge instructions.

Contact the hospital for advice if you have persistent vomiting, increasing pain, ongoing dizziness, difficulty swallowing or any symptom that concerns you after the procedure. Seek urgent medical attention immediately if you develop severe or worsening abdominal pain, heavy or persistent bleeding, fever, fainting, chest pain, difficulty breathing or signs of an allergic reaction.

What is a polyp, and why is it important to remove it?

A polyp is a small growth that develops on the lining of the colon. Most polyps do not cause symptoms, and many are harmless.

However, certain types of polyps can gradually develop into colorectal cancer over several years.

During a colonoscopy, the doctor can often remove these growths before they become cancerous. The removed tissue is then examined in a laboratory.

This is one of the ways colonoscopy can help prevent colorectal cancer.

Are endoscopy and colonoscopy safe?

Both procedures are generally safe, particularly when performed by experienced professionals.

Temporary bloating, mild abdominal discomfort or a sore throat may occur, depending on the procedure.

Rare complications include bleeding, a tear in the digestive tract or reactions to sedation. The risk varies according to your health, the procedure performed and whether treatment, such as polyp removal, is required.

Your doctor will explain the relevant risks and benefits before obtaining your consent.

After either procedure, seek urgent medical attention if you develop severe or worsening abdominal pain, heavy bleeding, fever, difficulty breathing or other concerning symptoms.

Will I receive my results immediately?

In many cases, the doctor will discuss the findings with you after the procedure.

If a biopsy or polyp has been removed, laboratory results may take several days or longer, depending on the tests required.

Your doctor will explain the findings and advise whether you need medication, further investigations, follow up or additional treatment.

Do not ignore persistent digestive symptoms

Many digestive conditions can be treated effectively, particularly when diagnosed early.

If you experience persistent digestive symptoms, unexplained anaemia, rectal bleeding or other concerning changes, consult your doctor to determine whether further investigation is necessary.

Do not wait for symptoms to become severe before seeking medical advice.
