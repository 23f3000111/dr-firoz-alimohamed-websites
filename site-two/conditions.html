Procedures that end the guessing

Bloating, heartburn, abdominal pain, constipation and jaundice all overlap. These are the examinations that tell them apart, and what each one can treat while it looks.

Where people start

The symptoms that bring people here

  • Bloating
  • Heartburn
  • Abdominal pain
  • Constipation
  • Jaundice
  • Difficulty swallowing
  • Blood in the stool
  • Unexplained weight loss
  • Chronic diarrhoea

Heartburn can be reflux or an ulcer. Bloating can be irritable bowel or something structural. The point of a specialist consultation is to tell them apart before treatment begins.

Procedures

Four examinations

Gastroscopy

A thin flexible camera passed through the mouth to examine the oesophagus, stomach and the first part of the small bowel. It answers questions about reflux, difficulty swallowing, ulcers, upper abdominal pain and unexplained anaemia, and takes the biopsies that confirm coeliac disease and H. pylori in the same pass.

  • Usually 10 to 20 minutes, under light sedation
  • Six hours without food beforehand
  • Biopsies taken where the lining looks abnormal
  • Findings explained the same day, in writing

Colonoscopy

Examination of the full length of the large bowel. It is the definitive test for rectal bleeding, a changed bowel habit, chronic diarrhoea and iron deficiency anaemia, and it is the screening test for bowel cancer. Polyps found during the examination are removed as they are found.

  • Usually 20 to 40 minutes, under light sedation
  • Bowel preparation the day before, and timing matters
  • Polyps removed during the same examination
  • Surveillance interval set before you leave

Therapeutic endoscopy

Where the camera treats as well as looks: stopping a bleeding ulcer or varix, removing polyps, stretching a narrowed oesophagus, placing stents, and relieving blocked bile ducts. This is the work that turns an emergency admission into a same day treatment.

  • Bleeding controlled at the point it is found
  • Narrowed passages dilated or stented
  • Polyps removed whole for laboratory examination
  • Often avoids open surgery entirely

Liver assessment

A structured assessment of abnormal liver blood tests or suspected liver disease: history, viral serology, metabolic screening, imaging, and liver biopsy where the diagnosis needs it. The aim is a named cause rather than a description, because viral hepatitis, fatty liver and drug related injury are managed in completely different ways.

  • Hepatitis B and C serology
  • Metabolic and alcohol related assessment
  • Ultrasound and further imaging as needed
  • Treatment and ongoing monitoring plan

Conditions

What these examinations find

Open sores in the lining of the stomach or duodenum, causing burning or gnawing upper abdominal pain that changes with meals. Most are caused by H. pylori infection or long term anti inflammatory tablets. Untreated they can bleed or perforate; treated, the great majority heal completely. Gastroscopy confirms the ulcer and tests for the organism at the same time.

Recurring abdominal pain with bloating and a changed bowel habit, where the bowel wall itself is normal. It is real and it is common, and it is diagnosed properly by first excluding coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease and other causes that look identical from the outside. Management combines diet, targeted medicines and review over time.

Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis inflame and ulcerate the bowel wall, producing diarrhoea, bleeding, urgency, pain and weight loss. Diagnosis needs colonoscopy with biopsies, and often imaging. Treatment aims at remission, getting inflammation to settle and keeping it settled, with monitoring rather than treatment only during flare ups.

Nearly all colon cancers begin as polyps that cause no symptoms for years. Colonoscopy finds them and removes them in the same examination, which is why screening prevents the disease rather than only detecting it. Screening usually starts at fifty, and earlier where a parent or sibling has been affected.

Inflammation of the liver from viral infection, alcohol, medicines or fat deposited in the liver. It is frequently silent, appearing first as an abnormal blood test rather than as jaundice. Establishing the cause is the whole task: viral hepatitis is treatable, fatty liver responds to metabolic management, and both do better found early.

Acid reflux and hiatus hernia, coeliac disease, gallstones and biliary pain, pancreatitis, difficulty swallowing, chronic constipation and diarrhoea, unexplained iron deficiency anaemia, and abnormal liver blood tests are all routine gastroenterology work. If your symptom is not listed here, it is still worth bringing.

Diagnosis and treatment in the same sitting

Therapeutic endoscopy stops bleeding, removes polyps and opens narrowed passages while the camera is already there.

Preparing

Before you come in

Before a gastroscopy

  1. Nothing to eat for six hours beforehand. Clear fluids may be allowed up to two hours before, and you will be told which.
  2. Keep taking your regular medicines unless told otherwise. Blood thinners and diabetes medicines need specific instructions.
  3. Bring someone who can take you home. Sedation makes driving unsafe for the rest of the day.
  4. Bring your current medicine list and any previous endoscopy or scan reports.

Before a colonoscopy

  1. Start the low residue diet on the day you are given, usually the day before.
  2. Take the bowel preparation exactly as timed. A clean bowel is what makes the examination accurate.
  3. Stay near a bathroom during preparation and keep drinking clear fluids so you do not become dehydrated.
  4. Arrange a companion for the journey home and plan a quiet rest of the day.

These are general guides. Your written instructions from the clinic always take priority, because preparation changes with the procedure and with the medicines you take.
