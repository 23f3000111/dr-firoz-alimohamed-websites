Colon cancer

Nearly all colon cancers begin as polyps, small growths that can cause no symptoms for years. Colonoscopy finds polyps and removes them, which is why screening prevents cancer rather than only detecting it.

What it is

Colon cancer starts in the lining of the large bowel or rectum. Most cases develop slowly from polyps over a number of years. The risk rises with age, with a close relative who has had bowel cancer or polyps, and with long standing inflammatory bowel disease.

Symptoms

  • Blood in the stool, or bleeding from the back passage
  • A lasting change in bowel habit
  • Abdominal pain or bloating that does not settle
  • Unexplained weight loss
  • Tiredness from iron deficiency anaemia

Early cancers and polyps often cause no symptoms at all, which is the reason for screening.

Screening and diagnosis

Colonoscopy examines the full length of the large bowel, removes polyps as they are found and takes biopsies of anything suspicious. Screening usually starts at fifty, and earlier where a close relative has been affected.

How it is treated

Polyps are removed during the colonoscopy and sent for analysis, and the next examination is timed according to what was found. If a cancer is confirmed, scans show how far it has spread and treatment is planned with surgical and oncology colleagues. Many early cancers can be cured with surgery.

Do not wait

Rectal bleeding or a changed bowel habit at any age should be assessed rather than watched.
