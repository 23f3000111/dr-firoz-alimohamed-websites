Peptic ulcers

Open sores in the lining of the stomach or the first part of the small bowel. Most have a clear cause, and the great majority heal completely once it is treated.

Patient brochurePDF download, available soon

What it is

A peptic ulcer forms when the protective lining of the stomach or duodenum breaks down and acid reaches the tissue beneath it. Two causes account for most ulcers: infection with H. pylori, a bacterium that lives in the stomach lining, and regular use of anti inflammatory painkillers such as ibuprofen, diclofenac or aspirin.

Symptoms

  • Gnawing or burning pain in the upper abdomen, often changing with meals
  • Pain that wakes you at night
  • Bloating, or feeling full after very little food
  • Nausea and loss of appetite

Some ulcers cause no pain at all and are only found when they bleed.

How it is found

Gastroscopy shows the ulcer directly and allows biopsies, both to test for H. pylori and to rule out anything more serious. Breath and stool tests can also detect H. pylori where a camera examination is not needed.

How it is treated

Medicine that reduces stomach acid gives the lining time to heal. H. pylori is cleared with a short course of antibiotics alongside that medicine, and a later test confirms it has gone. Anti inflammatory painkillers are stopped or replaced where possible. A bleeding ulcer can often be treated during the endoscopy itself.

If it is urgent

Vomiting blood, black tarry stools or sudden severe abdominal pain need same day assessment. Go to the hospital rather than waiting for a clinic appointment.
